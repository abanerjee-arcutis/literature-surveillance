    Level-1 keywords are the search terms. Each term is searched in every source that is switched on, and an article enters the review feed when it matches at least one term and no exclusion rule. Grey queries are derived from the PubMed query; type over one to set it yourself.
    Category sets the Product tag: Roflumilast if any matched term is Roflumilast, else Competitor, else Class.
    Level-2 intent says what an article is about, and feeds the Intent filter above the feeds. The title and the study type decide it: the checks run in a fixed order and the first match wins, so lab and animal studies are Mechanism, and an adverse event in the title makes even a review Safety. The abstract is read only when the title and study type say nothing, since nearly every abstract mentions both safety and efficacy. Hover an article's intent in a feed to see what decided it.
    Intent does not change what is searched or an article's priority. The rules are part of the job (INTENT_RULES in surveillance.py), so they are shown here but cannot be edited.
    Level-3 keywords set each article's priority; they do not widen the search, so an article still has to be found by an L1 keyword. They are grouped into reasons to read, learned from what was sent in the Articles of Interest emails. A reason matches when every keyword list under it has a word or phrase in the title (or in the title or abstract, where it says so) and none of its not when words is in the title. An article scores the points of its best reason, plus 5 for each further reason (up to 15 more), minus the points of any push-down rule. Keywords match ignoring case and punctuation, and words match as prefixes, so child finds children.

    How the Literature Surveillance works

    What this page is

    A daily scan of the dermatology literature for Medical Affairs. New articles are found every morning in PubMed and OpenAlex using the search terms in Settings → L1 keywords, scored for how useful they are likely to be, and sorted into three feeds. The same article found by several sources is shown once, with every source listed. Summaries are taken from each abstract or open-access full text in the authors' own words, so nothing is invented.

    How an article is scored

    Every article gets a score, never below 0, that suggests a reading order. The score is built from reasons to read, learned from what Medical Affairs actually sent in the Articles of Interest emails of January to October 2026.

    1. Find the reasons. The title (and for some reasons the abstract) is checked against 23 reasons to read. Each is worth points: own-product data, 100; comparisons of topical non-steroidals and guidelines, 85; competitor safety signals and topical steroid harms, 80; and 18 more down to 50. A reason can be switched off for an article by words that make it a poor fit, for example a transplant or alopecia context.
    2. Start from the best reason. The article's score begins with the points of its strongest reason.
    3. Add a little for each further reason. +5 for every other reason that applies, up to +15, so an article that is relevant in several ways rises slightly.
    4. Push down what was never sent. Points are taken off for kinds of article that the team did not send: congress abstracts (−40), Arcutis's own pivotal-trial papers (−40) and Arcutis-funded work (−35), oral or non-dermatology JAK inhibitor studies (−40), vitiligo and alopecia areata (−25), lab and veterinary studies (−30), and a few more. The score never goes below 0.
    5. Your own keywords. Keywords added in Settings → L3 count as one more reason, worth points.

    Example: a case series of topical roflumilast scores 100 for the own-product reason, plus 5 for a second reason (a safety signal) = 105, Must read. A numbered congress abstract that matches a 65-point reason scores 65 − 40 = 25, Low.

    Priority bands

    • Must read 85 or more: goes straight to the Accepted feed.
    • Of interest 60 to 84
    • FYI 40 to 59
    • Low under 40: folded away at the end of the Review feed.

    Checked against those same emails, 3 in 4 Must read articles had been sent, against 1 in 100 Low ones. Hover a priority, or open an article, to see exactly which reasons and push-downs produced its score. Settings → L3 lists every reason, its points and its keywords. The score ranks articles for reading; it does not judge the quality of the findings.

    The three feeds

    • Review feed: articles waiting for a decision, highest score first.
    • Accepted feed: articles worth passing on. Besides those a reviewer accepts, two kinds arrive by themselves: ✉ every article sent in an INFORM Articles of Interest email, and ★ every new Must read article.
    • Ignored feed: articles a reviewer ignored, and those the exclusion rules filter out (transplant and blood-cancer context, COPD and asthma, veterinary studies, oral JAK inhibitors, errata and retractions). Nothing is deleted; Restore sends an article back for review.

    Deciding

    The ⋮ menu on a row accepts or ignores an article. Either asks you to tick why, so the team can later see what is being sent and why. Every decision is saved with your name and the time, and a reviewer's decision always overrides the automatic ones. The message at the bottom of the page undoes your last move.

    Finding and sharing

    • Filters and search apply to all three feeds. Sort by priority, publication date or when the article was found; click the Published heading to flip the date order. New marks articles found in the last 7 days.
    • Bookmark (the ribbon on a row or an article page) keeps an article in your own Bookmarked tab, whichever feed it is on. Bookmarks are private to you.
    • Copy link on an article page links straight to that article.
    • Export copies the filtered list as an email digest or downloads it as a spreadsheet. For a weekly email, open the Accepted feed, pick Published: Last 30 days or Added: Last 7 days, then Export.
    How priority is set

    Priority is the L3 score (Settings → L3 priority keywords): the points of the article's best reason to read, plus 5 for each further reason, minus push-down points. It suggests a reading order; it does not weigh the findings.
